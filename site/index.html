DESIGN EXPLANATION / WHITE HAT

Making the comparison visible

This visualization uses the user-provided OECD Air emissions — Greenhouse gas emissions Inventories export. It shows aggregate greenhouse gases for the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Canada, Australia, and Japan between 1990 and 2018. These six countries were selected to compare contrasting trajectories among major industrial economies with complete annual records. They are a purposive sample, not a representative sample of the world or a ranking of all OECD members.

The default view computes each annual value as a percentage change from that country’s own 1990 emissions: (annual emissions / 1990 emissions − 1) × 100. This common baseline makes relative changes comparable despite differences in national size. A second view shows absolute annual emissions in million tonnes of carbon-dioxide equivalent (Mt CO₂e), with a zero-based vertical axis. Switching between the views exposes the difference between relative progress and the scale of emissions. Neither measure establishes per-person responsibility, efficiency, or historical cumulative contribution.

Lines preserve chronological order, a dashed reference marks the baseline, and the country controls let readers reduce overlap. Each selected country also has a labeled endpoint summary, so the chart does not rely on color alone. The annual data table exposes exact values and the source’s observation status. All available years in the stated interval remain visible; the countries share one vertical scale within each view. That scale stays fixed when countries are hidden, preventing selection from silently magnifying changes.

All observations use total emissions excluding land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF). Source unit multipliers are applied before conversion to Mt CO₂e. We do not sum regional aggregates, mix individual gases with their aggregate, or substitute zeros for missing values. These are inventory observations from a newer export and may differ from earlier OECD releases. National inventories also omit some dimensions of climate responsibility, including emissions embodied in imported goods. The chart describes reported trajectories; it does not attribute changes to a particular policy.

Behind the framing: inspect the manipulation

The campaign above is intentionally misleading. Its numbers are genuine. Its framing is selective.

01 / Select the starting point

2007 is the U.S. peak within 1990–2018. Starting there highlights a 10.3% decline; the full period shows a 3.3% increase.

02 / Magnify the slope

The vertical axis starts at 6,000 Mt instead of zero. This makes the decline occupy much more of the chart’s height.

03 / Broaden the claim

“A cleaner future” implies durable success beyond what a selected historical emissions interval can establish.

Showing the selective 2007–2018 window with a truncated axis.

DESIGN EXPLANATION / BLACK HAT

True values, inflated reassurance

This presentation uses the same OECD aggregate greenhouse gas series and the same definition of total emissions excluding LULUCF as the white hat visualization. No observation is fabricated or numerically altered. Instead, the campaign constructs a reassuring account of American climate progress through three coordinated decisions: selective filtering, a compressed vertical range, and an overconfident headline.

First, it begins in 2007 rather than 1990. In this export, 2007 is the highest U.S. annual total within the assignment’s 1990–2018 interval. Comparing that peak with 2018 produces a decline of about 10.3%. This is a valid calculation, but it favors the desired narrative: comparing 1990 with 2018 instead produces an increase of about 3.3%. The visible date label gives the presentation surface credibility while leaving readers to discover why that particular starting point matters. Isolating one country also removes the contrasting trajectories visible in the comparative view.

Second, the vertical scale starts at 6,000 Mt CO₂e rather than zero. A nonzero baseline is not inherently inappropriate for a line chart, particularly when examining small fluctuations. Here it is deliberately combined with a progress narrative and a filled area to enlarge the visual impression of improvement. The axis remains labeled, making the tactic detectable through careful reading without presenting an obviously broken chart. The filled region represents height above the truncated baseline, not the full quantity of emissions.

Third, the headline turns a historical change into a broad claim about a cleaner future. Neither this interval nor this inventory establishes future emissions, policy effectiveness, or comprehensive environmental improvement. The large percentage, green palette, and restrained typography make the message feel confident and authoritative. These choices mislead through data transformation, visual encoding, and titles. The separate explanation and context-restoration control expose the tactics for this educational exercise. Restoring the full period and a zero baseline demonstrates that the evidence supports a more qualified conclusion than the campaign suggests.
